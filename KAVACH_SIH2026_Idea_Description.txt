SECTION 1: PROBLEM ANALYSIS

1.1 The magnitude of the threat

The expansion of digital technology in India has made its networks among the most frequently targeted in the world. The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) handled 15.92 lakh cyber security incidents in 2023, 20.41 lakh in 2024 and 29.44 lakh in 2025, according to figures presented to the Lok Sabha by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology. That is an increase of 85% in two years, or about 8,000 incidents every day. Behind every incident is a network that was scanned, entered or disrupted, and a defender who found out too late.

The financial cost is rising just as steeply. According to IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026, the average cost of a data breach in India has reached a record ₹25.5 crore, up 15.9% from ₹22 crore in 2025. The same report shows the value of intelligent defence: organisations that used no AI or security automation paid an average of ₹31.6 crore per breach, while those that used them extensively limited the damage to ₹21.3 crore, a saving of over ₹10 crore per incident. Yet only 32% of Indian organisations reported extensive AI and automation in their security operations. The protective technology exists; its adoption, and the trust placed in it in sensitive settings, remain low.

Speed is the decisive factor. The CrowdStrike 2026 Global Threat Report found that the average eCrime "breakout time", the period between an adversary's first foothold and their first lateral movement, was only 29 minutes, 65% faster than the previous year. The fastest recorded breakout took 27 seconds, and in one case data exfiltration began within four minutes. By contrast, IBM reports that Indian organisations without automation take on average 236 days to detect a breach and a further 75 days to contain it. The gap between a 29-minute attack and a 236-day detection process is the central failure this proposal sets out to close.

1.2 Why existing defences do not work

India does not lack security tools; the problem is that most of them rest on assumptions attackers have already overcome. We have identified six gaps that current solutions do not address.

First, detection is static and reactive. Traditional machine-learning intrusion detection evaluates each network flow on its own and labels it benign or malicious. As the problem statement points out, this ignores the temporal and causal structure of an attack: the order in which ports are scanned, the way SYN flags precede ACK floods, and the timing of reconnaissance before lateral movement. An attack is a process that unfolds over time, not a single unusual packet. A classifier that only asks "is this flow bad?" will always answer after the attacker has acted.

Second, there is no foresight. Current systems report what has already happened; none of the widely used tools can tell a defender that the reconnaissance scan seen now is likely to become an initial-access attempt within minutes. Without forecasting, analysts cannot prioritise, and the 29-minute breakout window closes before a human can respond.

Third, black-box AI cannot be trusted with national-security decisions. Where AI is used, it usually produces a score with no explanation. A specialist at a power utility or strategic establishment cannot justify isolating a production system on the basis of an unexplained model output, nor defend that action in an after-action review. The problem statement is clear that "black-box outputs without interpretability are not acceptable", yet this is still how most commercial products behave.

Fourth, single-level telemetry creates blind spots. Flow-based tools see volume but miss slow, low-rate reconnaissance deliberately paced to stay below flow thresholds. Packet-based tools see timing and flags but lose the wider behavioural picture. Few systems combine both, leaving exploitable gaps between them.

Fifth, cloud dependence excludes the most important networks. Most modern AI security platforms rely on the vendor's cloud for inference, updates and threat intelligence, so air-gapped networks in the strategic, space, nuclear and classified government sectors cannot use them. The networks that most need advanced AI defence are precisely the ones denied it.

Sixth, the defence itself can be compromised. Logs can be altered, evidence deleted and AI models corrupted through manipulated analyst feedback. An insider, or an adversary already inside, can erase their traces. Current tools offer little assurance that their own records, models and decisions have not been tampered with.

1.3 Quantifying the scope

The whole digital economy is affected, but the stakes are highest for Critical Information Infrastructure (CII). The National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC), established under Section 70A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 and operating under NTRO, identifies six critical sectors: Power and Energy; Banking, Financial Services and Insurance; Telecom; Transport; Government; and Strategic and Public Enterprises. IBM's 2025 findings for India show some of the highest per-breach costs in exactly these environments: research organisations averaged ₹28.9 crore, transportation ₹28.8 crore and industrial organisations ₹26.4 crore per breach.

The loss of analyst time is just as serious. A Security Operations Centre (SOC) analyst facing thousands of uncorrelated, unexplained alerts spends most of the shift on noise. Our own benchmark shows this clearly: a conventional logistic-regression detector applied to the same traffic wrongly flags 29% of normal windows as attacks. At national scale, this rate of false positives wastes thousands of analyst-hours every day and buries genuine attacks.

1.4 Alignment with national priorities

The problem sits at the intersection of several national initiatives:

• The National Cyber Security Policy, 2013, which calls for protecting CII through indigenous capability.
• Sections 70 and 70A of the IT Act, 2000, which allow systems to be declared Protected Systems and established NCIIPC to protect them.
• CERT-In's Directions of April 2022, which require incidents to be reported within six hours, a deadline that is unrealistic without automated, explainable detection.
• The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, which raises the legal and financial consequences of breaches involving personal data.
• The IndiaAI Mission, 2024, with an outlay of more than ₹10,000 crore focused on trustworthy, indigenous AI.
• Atmanirbhar Bharat, which calls for sovereign technology in strategic sectors, free of dependence on foreign cloud services.

1.5 The urgency

Every trend points the same way: attacks are becoming faster, costlier and more frequent, while the most important networks still rely on reactive, opaque or cloud-dependent tools. India needs a defence system that learns how its networks behave, predicts where an attack is heading, explains each of its decisions, runs completely offline and cannot be quietly compromised from within.

Sources: CERT-In incident data (MeitY written reply to the Lok Sabha, 2025); IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 and 2026, India findings; CrowdStrike 2026 Global Threat Report; Information Technology Act, 2000, Sections 70 and 70A.

KAVACH closes the gap between detecting an attack that has already happened and anticipating the one that is coming.

SECTION 2: PROPOSED SOLUTION AND INNOVATION

2.1 Unique value proposition

KAVACH is an offline AI defence system that learns how a network behaves, predicts where an attack is heading before it is carried out, explains each of its decisions, and records everything on a tamper-evident blockchain ledger, so that India's critical networks are protected by AI that is predictive, transparent and trustworthy by design.

2.2 The key innovation: from classification to world modelling

Traditional intrusion detection asks one question: is this traffic malicious? KAVACH asks a different one: given how the network is behaving now, what state will it be in next, and is that trajectory leading towards compromise?

To answer it, we developed NetJEPA, an adaptation of the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) for networks. NetJEPA does not memorise signatures; it learns an internal model of how network states change over time, P(S_t+1 | S_t), which is exactly the capability the problem statement asks for. It works in four steps:

1. Each 5-second window of traffic is represented as a graph: hosts and network segments are nodes, communication patterns are edges, and every node carries both flow-level and packet-level features.
2. A Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT) encodes each graph, and a temporal encoder (GRU) reads the last three windows in order, producing a compact latent state that captures both the network's structure and its recent history.
3. The next latent state is predicted as a full probability distribution (a mean and a variance) rather than a single point, and Monte-Carlo rollouts of this distribution simulate K steps into the future.
4. Each current and predicted state is mapped to an infiltration probability and a MITRE ATT&CK stage.

We evaluated this design on 8,043 held-out windows of real CIC-IDS-2017 traffic, drawn from later in each capture than the training data, against the logistic-regression baseline required by the problem statement:

• F1 score: 0.86 for KAVACH against 0.49 for the baseline, an improvement of about 76%.
• ROC AUC: 0.96 against 0.73, an improvement of about 31%.
• False-positive rate: 6.3% against 29%, roughly a quarter as many false alarms.
• Forecasting one to three windows ahead: AUC of 0.96 to 0.97, against 0.73 to 0.74 when the baseline's current score is used as the forecast.

One result stood out: when the baseline was given the same three-window history as NetJEPA, it did not improve at all (F1 0.46). The gain therefore does not come from seeing more data; it comes from learning how traffic evolves over time, which is the evidence the problem statement asks for.

Several other things set KAVACH apart:

• It learns from flow and packet features together. On raw packet captures this produced an F1 score of 0.87, against 0.62 with flow features alone and 0.61 with packet features alone.
• Its transitions are learned with supervision. The dataset's published attack timeline supervised the model's predictions of the future as well as the present, which is why its forecasts are as accurate as its detections.
• A blockchain trust layer ensures that the AI's outputs, the model itself and its training data cannot be altered without the change being visible, addressing insider tampering that ordinary tools overlook.

2.3 Key features and how each maps to the problem statement

Feature 1: Reading both flow and packet traffic (PS: "Teams must work with both flow-level and packet-level features")

We built a streaming reader for PCAP and PCAPNG files that ran 58 times faster than the Scapy-based path in our tests, falling back to Scapy for unusual link types. KAVACH also reads flow records in the CIC-IDS-2017, CSE-CIC-IDS-2018 and CTU-13 formats and detects the format automatically. For each 5-second window it extracts 16 flow-level features (TCP flag counts, bytes and packets per flow, duration, inter-arrival time statistics, forward/backward ratios and protocol mix) and 11 packet-level features (TTL mean and variance, TCP window size, fragment flags, payload size spread, port-scan signatures and retransmission counts), matching the attributes listed in the problem statement.

Feature 2: Forecasting with an honest sense of uncertainty (PS: "Forecast future network states and estimate the probability of attacker progression")

For every window KAVACH reports the current infiltration probability, a forecast for the next three windows within a 10th–90th percentile band, the probability of an attack within those windows, and the probability that the attack moves to its next stage. It can also anticipate attacks before they begin: for windows that look normal now but are attacked shortly after, it achieves an AUC of 0.90 to 0.93. Its uncertainty is reliable too: the 90% prediction intervals contained the actual next state 95% of the time.

Feature 3: MITRE ATT&CK stages and attack chains (PS: "Map predicted behaviour to recognised attack stages")

Current and predicted states are labelled as Reconnaissance, Initial Access, Lateral Movement, Command and Control or Exfiltration. Stage classification reaches a macro-F1 of 0.75, performing especially well on Reconnaissance (F1 0.91) and Initial Access (F1 0.86). Related windows are then linked into a single timeline, so the analyst sees one incident evolving rather than hundreds of separate alerts.

Feature 4: Explanations at six levels (PS: "Black-box outputs without interpretability are not acceptable")

We invested heavily here, because this is where most AI security tools fail. Every alert in KAVACH can be explained in six ways:

1. Integrated Gradients, measured against a normal-traffic baseline, show which flags, ports and flow statistics pushed the score up.
2. Learned concepts from a sparse autoencoder with 1,231 active features, 88 of them attack-specific, show which patterns the model recognised.
3. Causal tracing identifies which part of the model actually made the decision.
4. A tuned lens shows how confidence built up layer by layer, from an AUC of 0.77 on the raw features to 0.96 at the output.
5. Linear probes show what each layer knows about scanning, escalation and whether an attack is imminent (up to 0.93 balanced accuracy).
6. A concept bottleneck expresses the model's reasoning as ten human-readable concepts; an analyst can override any of them and immediately see how the verdict changes.

Feature 5: A response plan, and a record nobody can quietly change (PS: "interpretable decision support for defenders")

Each alert comes with a prioritised plan covering immediate, short-term and ongoing actions and what to investigate, with a ready-to-run command and the matching MITRE mitigation for each step. Every prediction, model update, training dataset and analyst action is hash-chained on a blockchain ledger; low-risk events are batched together, while high-risk events get their own detailed blocks. Smart contracts enforce three rules: an unacknowledged attack is escalated automatically; blocking an IP address requires agreement from at least three nodes; and a new model that performs worse than the previous one is rolled back automatically.

Feature 6: Security that scales with network sensitivity (PS: "applicability to enterprise environments and Critical Information Infrastructure")

Different networks need different levels of control, so KAVACH runs a separate node for each zone and tightens both sign-in and response authority as sensitivity rises. This is implemented in the prototype:

• Routine zones (office and administrative networks): password sign-in; approved responses execute automatically.
• Sensitive zones (finance, procurement and personnel systems): password plus a one-time authenticator code; every action must be confirmed by an analyst.
• Mission-critical zones (classified research, defence and grid-control networks): password, authenticator code and a hardware security key; every action needs approval from a second, independent officer, or can be escalated.

Every sign-in, failure and lockout is recorded on the ledger. Threat indicators flow upward from lower zones to higher ones, never downward.

2.4 A day with KAVACH: the user journey

Consider an analyst in a telecom operator's network operations centre.

1. Signing in. The analyst logs in to the offline KAVACH console using the method their zone requires (see Feature 6). The login is recorded on the ledger.
2. Watching. KAVACH receives a copy of network traffic through a TAP or mirror port, or works on an uploaded capture, and the Operations dashboard updates risk scores window by window.
3. Early warning. At 10:02 the risk line starts to rise because one host is sweeping destination ports. KAVACH labels this Reconnaissance, and the forecast gives an 80% chance of progression to the next stage within three windows. The analyst knows before the breakout, not after.
4. Understanding. Opening the alert, the analyst sees that a sudden jump in unique destination ports and in flows from a single source drove the decision, that the model recognised a scanning pattern, and how its confidence grew.
5. Acting. The response panel recommends blocking the scanning source at the perimeter, checking exposed services and enabling extra logging, with commands already filled in. The analyst confirms. In a mission-critical zone, a second officer must approve before anything is enforced.
6. Proving it later. The decision, the action and the model version that raised the alert are chained on the ledger, so weeks later an auditor can verify cryptographically that nothing was altered.
7. Improving. The analyst's confirmed verdicts are checked for signs of feedback poisoning and then used to retrain the model safely, so it improves without becoming a way in for attackers.

Going from the first odd-looking window to a documented, explained response takes minutes, against the industry average of 236 days just to identify a breach.

2.5 Fitting in with existing infrastructure

KAVACH is designed to sit alongside existing security infrastructure, not replace it:

• It is deployed passively on a SPAN or mirror port or a TAP and never sits in the path of live traffic, so if it fails the network keeps running.
• It accepts standard inputs: PCAP captures, NetFlow/IPFIX-style flow records and the main public dataset formats.
• It uses the organisation's existing on-premises firewall for enforcement: KAVACH decides, the firewall acts.
• Its REST and WebSocket API (FastAPI) lets detections, forecasts, explanations and ledger evidence flow into an existing SIEM or SOC workflow.
• Sites can share threat intelligence without a network link: signed ledger bundles are exported and imported on secure removable media, so air-gapped sites and sectoral CERTs can share what they have learned.

2.6 From prototype to national scale

Stage 1, the prototype, exists today. It runs on a single machine and includes ingestion, the NetJEPA model, six explanation methods, the response engine, the ledger, three-tier access control, a web dashboard and a command-line tool. The model has about 1.3 million parameters (around 5 MB), trains in roughly 21 minutes on two CPU cores and needs no GPU.

Stage 2 is a pilot at one CII organisation, with a separate node per sensitivity zone, fine-tuning on that organisation's own traffic, and integration with its firewall and SIEM.

Stage 3 is a sector-wide rollout, with organisations linked through a permissioned multi-node ledger, sharing verified threat indicators while raw traffic stays where it is.

Stage 4 is a national network of sector deployments working with NCIIPC and CERT-In, where an early warning at one site helps protect all others, with no data leaving sovereign, air-gapped infrastructure.

2.7 Where it would be used

• Telecom core networks: early detection of reconnaissance and command-and-control beaconing across operator backbones, with forecasts that help teams focus on the few threats likely to escalate.
• Power and energy: protecting the IT networks adjacent to grid control systems, where explainable, approval-gated responses matter more than full automation.
• Banking and finance: predicting how brute-force and web attacks on payment and core-banking systems are likely to develop, with an evidence trail regulators can audit.
• Government networks: fewer unnecessary alerts, and incidents that are already documented and explained, helping meet CERT-In's six-hour reporting requirement.

2.8 How KAVACH compares with existing options

Signature-based intrusion detection systems, traditional ML classifiers and cloud AI platforms each cover part of the picture; none combines what KAVACH does. KAVACH learns how the network behaves over time, forecasts attack progression with an estimate of uncertainty, maps both current and predicted states to MITRE ATT&CK, explains its decisions at six levels that analysts can correct, and learns from flow and packet features together. It also runs fully air-gapped on CPUs, keeps a tamper-evident record of its own decisions and has no licensing cost. Cloud platforms come closest on analytics but cannot run in an air gap and offer only limited, vendor-controlled explanations. Classic IDS and ML classifiers can run offline, but they judge traffic one flow at a time and make no forecasts.

2.9 The principles behind our design

Four ideas shaped almost every decision. To be predictive rather than merely detective, the unit we analyse is a sequence of network states, not a single flow. Every alert must be explainable, because in national security an alert nobody can explain is an alert nobody will act on. The system trusts nothing silently: its outputs, models and training data receive the same tamper evidence as the networks it watches. And it is sovereign from the start: everything runs on ordinary on-premises hardware, with no dependence on foreign cloud services.

2.10 Intended users

• SOC analysts and incident responders in NCIIPC-notified CII organisations: telecom operators, power utilities, banks, transport operators, government departments and strategic public-sector enterprises.
• National agencies such as NTRO/NCIIPC, CERT-In and sectoral CERTs, which gain verifiable incident evidence and offline threat-sharing.
• Defence and strategic establishments running air-gapped networks that cannot use cloud AI.
• Enterprise security teams, CISOs and auditors who need calibrated risk scores and proof that security records have not been altered.

In short, KAVACH lets a network see its own threats coming. The next section explains how we built it to do so reliably.

SECTION 3: TECHNICAL IMPLEMENTATION

3.1 Why we chose each technology

Every technology choice had to meet three rules: open-source, fully offline, and able to run on standard hardware without a GPU.

• Python 3 for the core: the standard language of AI and security research, with a large open-source ecosystem, and easy for government teams to read, audit and maintain.
• PyTorch for deep learning: mature, open-source and efficient on CPUs, letting us build the probabilistic predictor and gradient-based explanation methods as intended.
• PyTorch Geometric (HGTConv) for graph learning: Heterogeneous Graph Transformers handle different node types (hosts, network segments) and link types, just as real networks are structured.
• A custom struct-based packet parser with Scapy as a fallback: it streams very large captures without loading them into memory and ran 58 times faster than the Scapy path in our tests, while Scapy, named in the problem statement, remains available as a safeguard.
• NumPy and pandas for data processing: vectorised feature engineering over millions of flow records.
• scikit-learn for baselines and analysis: the logistic-regression baseline, linear probes, the tuned-lens ridge regression and calibration metrics.
• FastAPI with Uvicorn for the backend: asynchronous REST and WebSocket handling, streaming results one window at a time.
• SQLite in WAL mode for storage: no administration, a single file, fully offline, suited to an air-gapped node.
• A hash-chained ledger with Merkle batching and Python smart contracts for the blockchain layer: lightweight and built for air gaps, and structured after permissioned ledgers such as Hyperledger Fabric, which we plan to use for multi-site deployment.
• React, TypeScript, Vite and Tailwind CSS for the front end: a responsive, type-safe dashboard that makes no external requests, with fonts and assets bundled locally.

3.2 System architecture

KAVACH is organised into seven layers, each with one job:

1. Ingestion reads packet captures and flow logs, detects the dataset format and splits traffic into 5-second windows at both flow and packet level.
2. The graph builder turns each window into a graph of network segments, hosts and connections, with a 29-value feature vector (16 flow features, 11 packet features and two flags showing which kinds of data were available).
3. The NetJEPA world model combines a two-layer HGT for each graph, a two-layer GRU over the last three windows, and a predictor that outputs the mean and variance of the next state. During training, a slowly updated copy of the encoder (an exponential moving average) and a VICReg objective prevent the internal representation from collapsing.
4. The prediction heads, a calibrated infiltration head and a MITRE ATT&CK stage classifier, are applied to the current state and to each predicted future state.
5. The explainability engine runs the six explanation methods on the same inference path as the live system, so an explanation always matches the prediction the analyst sees.
6. The response and trust layer covers attack-chain reconstruction, stage-specific playbooks, smart contracts, three-tier access control and the ledger.
7. The interface layer consists of the FastAPI backend, the React dashboard and the command-line tool.

The whole system sits inside a single air-gapped boundary, and no component makes outgoing network calls.

3.3 How data moves through the system

Input consists of raw packet captures (PCAP or PCAPNG) from a passive TAP, a mirror port or an uploaded file, and flow records in CIC-IDS-2017, CSE-CIC-IDS-2018 or CTU-13 format. The prototype was trained and validated on the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset from the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity, using both its flow records and raw packet captures. Together these cover 14 attack types across 7 categories: DoS, DDoS, port scanning, brute force, web attacks, botnets and infiltration.

Processing steps:

1. Parsing: the streaming reader extracts IP addresses, ports, protocol, TTL, TCP window, flags, fragment bits, payload size and retransmission status from each packet.
2. Windowing: packets and flows are grouped into 5-second windows. Flow statistics use IPFIX-style timeouts equal to the window length, so the flow and packet views describe exactly the same period.
3. Normalisation: features are standardised per capture, count features are log-scaled, and flags and ratios are left unchanged.
4. Labelling (training only): attack windows are labelled from the dataset's published attack schedule, including attacker and victim addresses. In doing this we found and fixed a bug that had mislabelled about half the attack windows on two capture days; all results in this document use the corrected labels.
5. Graph construction and encoding: each window becomes a graph, and NetJEPA summarises the last three windows into a latent state.
6. Forecasting: the predictor draws 64 samples at each step and rolls forward three steps, producing a range of possible futures for both infiltration and attack stage.
7. Calibration: temperature scaling, fitted on a separate calibration set, ensures that a displayed 80% risk really means about 80%. It reduced the expected calibration error from 0.049 to 0.033.
8. Explanation and response: attributions, concepts and playbooks are generated, and the result is written to the ledger according to its risk tier.

For every window the output includes the calibrated infiltration probability, the MITRE stage and model confidence, a three-step forecast with its uncertainty band and progression probability, the features that drove the result, a prioritised response plan and a ledger receipt. Results stream live to the dashboard over WebSocket, are stored in the results database and are available through the API and the command-line tool.

3.4 Interfaces and integration

KAVACH exposes a documented REST and WebSocket API so it can plug into existing SOC tools:

• POST /ingest/upload and WS /ws/pipeline to submit a capture and receive results as each window is processed.
• GET /flows and GET /flows/{id} to search and filter scored windows by risk, stage, source or time.
• GET /explain/{id} and POST /explain/{id}/concepts for detailed explanations and analyst corrections.
• GET /attack-chain/{id} for the reconstructed incident timeline and its remediation plan.
• POST /feedback to record analyst verdicts, which are chained on the ledger.
• /auth endpoints for tiered sign-in, and approval endpoints for the two-person rule in mission-critical zones.
• GET /benchmark and GET /provenance for model performance and the lineage of the model and its data, for audits.
• Blockchain endpoints to verify the chain, retrieve cryptographic evidence for any prediction, and export or import signed bundles to synchronise air-gapped sites.
• WS /ws/replay to replay previous captures for training and forensic analysis.

Integration with on-premises systems is deliberately limited to network TAPs and packet brokers on the input side, the organisation's own firewall for enforcement, and SIEM platforms through the API. Nothing depends on the internet or any cloud service.

3.5 Security, privacy and compliance

• Air-gapped by design: no outgoing connections, telemetry or licence checks. The system behaves identically with the network cable removed.
• Data minimisation: KAVACH examines packet headers and statistics, not payload content, and raw traffic never leaves the local node. Only derived indicators, such as attacker IP addresses and pattern hashes, are shared between sites.
• Tamper-evident audit trail: every prediction, every model load (weights are hashed on each load), every training-data manifest and every analyst action is hash-chained. Low-risk periods are batched under Merkle roots for efficiency, while high-risk periods get complete blocks with hashes of their features, graph and attributions. Any edit or deletion is detected when the chain is verified.
• Model integrity: the model registry rejects incompatible checkpoints, verifies training data against its chained manifest, and automatically reverts to the previous model if the new one's false-positive rate on reviewed alerts is more than double the old one's.
• Protection against feedback poisoning: analyst verdicts are checked for suspicious volume, patterns and timing before they are used for retraining.
• Access matched to sensitivity: sign-in and approval rules tighten from zone to zone (see Feature 6); passwords are securely hashed, five failed attempts lock the account, and every login is traceable to a named person.
• Compliance: KAVACH follows NCIIPC guidance for protected systems, supports CERT-In's incident-reporting directions with timestamped, explained incident records available well within six hours, and applies the data-minimisation principles of the DPDP Act, 2023.

3.6 Interface design

The interface is designed for someone working under pressure: the analyst should see the evidence before being asked to act, and nothing should depend on anything outside the network.

• The Operations dashboard shows a live risk timeline, the current zone and its response rule, and a MITRE ATT&CK matrix, giving an at-a-glance view of the network.
• The Flow Detail view shows, for any window, the forecast band, stage breakdown, the features behind the decision and deeper explanations, one click from the alert.
• The Attack Chain view presents related windows as a single incident with its response plan.
• The Network view shows every observed host as a node and every conversation as a link, coloured by measured risk.
• The Forensics and Provenance pages cover ledger verification, a tamper demonstration, evidence retrieval and model lineage.
• The Benchmark page shows the latest validation results, including where the model is weaker, so decision-makers can see exactly how well it works.
• The command-line tool provides predict, benchmark, train, verify-chain and info commands for scripted use, server rooms or fully disconnected machines.

The dashboard is served locally by the KAVACH node, so any workstation inside the secure network can open it in a browser with nothing to install, and it works completely offline.

3.7 Development and testing

• Git for version control, with fixed random seeds, the training configuration saved with every checkpoint, and a hash of the training data chained to the ledger before each training run, so every result can be reproduced.
• 110 automated tests covering the parsers, feature extraction, graph building, the model, calibration, ledger tamper and deletion detection, Merkle batching, smart contracts, the feedback audit, tiered sign-in and two-person approval, and all API endpoints. Tests use isolated copies of the ledger and databases, so no real data is affected.
• Vite and TypeScript for fast front-end builds with type checking.
• A single command runs the full benchmark: baselines, calibration, flow-versus-packet comparisons, temporal-context comparisons, leave-one-attack-out generalisation tests, and adversarial robustness and interpretability checks.

The architecture works as a complete system; the next section shows that it can be delivered, deployed and sustained.

SECTION 4: FEASIBILITY AND IMPACT ASSESSMENT

4.1 We are starting from a working system

KAVACH already exists and works. Ingestion, the NetJEPA world model, forecasting, the six explanation methods, the response engine, the blockchain ledger, three-tier access control, the web dashboard and the command-line tool are all built, trained on real traffic and covered by 110 passing tests. Our plan for the 36 hours of the hackathon is therefore a focused sprint to strengthen, extend and demonstrate a running system, not a rush to build one from scratch.

• Hours 0–3: set up the environment, check the datasets and re-run the benchmark, ending with a reproducible environment whose results match this document.
• Hours 3–9: harden the response engine so it identifies attacker and victim from traffic direction and exports firewall rules that expire automatically, ending with accurate, enforceable and reversible actions.
• Hours 9–15: extend three-tier access with production-grade identity integration and upward-only sharing of threat indicators between zone nodes.
• Hours 15–21: validate across datasets using CSE-CIC-IDS-2018 and CTU-13 (the parsers are already written) and add an LSTM baseline for comparison, ending with a broader benchmark.
• Hours 21–27: improve robustness with a noise-augmented training run and a lightweight rule layer for scan and flood patterns, ending with a model that resists manipulation better and catches more unfamiliar attack families.
• Hours 27–32: integration testing, the full test suite and dashboard polish, ending with a stable end-to-end demo.
• Hours 32–36: rehearse the demo and finalise documentation, ending with the demo video, architecture document and README.

4.4 The difference it makes, in numbers

Unless stated otherwise, these figures come from the 8,043 held-out validation windows:

• Better detection: F1 rises from 0.49 with the logistic-regression baseline to 0.86 with KAVACH, about 76% higher, and ROC AUC rises from 0.73 to 0.96.
• 78% fewer false alarms: on normal traffic, false alerts fall from 1,568 to 339 (a false-positive rate of 6.3% instead of 29%), directly reducing analyst triage time.
• Advance warning: attacks are forecast one to three windows ahead with an AUC of 0.96 to 0.97; attack onsets, where a window looks normal now but an attack follows shortly after, are forecast with an AUC of 0.90 to 0.93.
• Both traffic levels matter: on raw captures, flow and packet features together give an F1 of 0.87, against 0.62 and 0.61 for either alone.
• Learning over time helps: with identical training, a three-window history beats a single window (F1 0.77 against 0.74), while the baseline gains nothing from the same history.
• Reliable confidence: calibration error falls from 0.049 to 0.033, and 90% prediction intervals contain the true next state 95% of the time.
• Faster ingestion: our parser read packet captures 58 times faster than the Scapy-based path in our tests.
• Small and light: the model is about 5 MB with 1.3 million parameters and runs without a GPU on hardware organisations already own.

4.5 Challenges and how we will handle them

A proposal like this should be honest about what does not yet work, so here are the main challenges and our plan for each.

Challenge 1: attack families the model has never seen. The problem statement asks for generalisation to unseen attacks, so we tested it directly: for each attack family, we retrained the model with that family removed and evaluated it on that family. KAVACH handled unseen DDoS very well (AUC 0.98, 95% of attacks caught) because it had learned related DoS behaviour, but struggled with families unlike anything it had seen, such as port scanning (AUC 0.58).
Our plan: add layers of detection. NetJEPA will work alongside a transparent rule layer for scanning, flooding and brute-force patterns and per-host statistical baselines. We will also train on more varied data (CSE-CIC-IDS-2018, CTU-13 and each site's own traffic) and keep learning from verified analyst feedback.

Challenge 2: attackers trying to evade the model. Our robustness tests showed that small, targeted changes to certain features can flip some detections.
Our plan: train with added noise and adversarial examples, and rely on layered detection so an attacker must fool several independent detectors at once. In real traffic, hiding features such as port fan-out also means carrying out less of the attack.

Challenge 3: some attack stages are rare in the data. Public datasets contain little lateral-movement and exfiltration traffic.
Our plan: add datasets, capture red-team exercise traffic during the pilot, and fine-tune on data from each sector.

Challenge 4: lab traffic is not real traffic. Public datasets are generated in testbeds.
Our plan: run a calibration phase at every pilot site, refitting normalisation, temperature and thresholds on that site's normal traffic, and monitor performance continuously on the Benchmark page.

Challenge 5: scaling the ledger to many sites. The prototype uses a local ledger with a single writer.
Our plan: move to a permissioned multi-node ledger (for example Hyperledger Fabric with Raft ordering), give each node its own signing key, and periodically anchor Merkle roots to offline write-once storage.

Challenge 6: getting analysts to trust AI. Security teams are understandably wary of it.
Our plan: rely on six-layer explanations, calibrated confidence, human approval steps and the ability to override the model's concepts. All of these keep people in control, which is what earns trust in high-stakes settings.

4.6 After the hackathon

• Months 0–3, hardening: layered detection, adversarial training, cross-dataset validation, production-grade identity integration and a replicated database.
• Months 3–6, pilot: deployment at one CII organisation, for example a telecom or power-sector partner, in passive monitoring mode, with site calibration and analyst feedback loops.
• Months 6–12, multi-zone rollout: independent nodes per sensitivity zone, one-way sharing of threat indicators between zones, a multi-node permissioned ledger, and alignment with NCIIPC's protected-system audit process.
• Months 12–24, sector and national scale: federation across organisations within a sector, integration with sectoral CERTs and CERT-In reporting, and support for industrial control protocols (Modbus, DNP3) to extend coverage to operational-technology networks.

4.7 Costs and benefits

Cost per node (indicative):
• Software licences: none, as everything is open-source.
• Hardware: one ordinary server without a GPU, typically a few lakh rupees and often covered by servers the organisation already owns.
• Cloud and data transfer: none, as it runs offline.
• Operations: occasional CPU retraining (about 20 minutes), which existing SOC staff can handle.

What it gives back:
• IBM's 2026 findings for India put the average breach at ₹31.6 crore without AI and automation and ₹21.3 crore with extensive AI and automation, a difference of about ₹10.3 crore per breach. Preventing or shortening even one breach over the system's life would repay its deployment many times over.
• With 78% fewer false alarms, analysts regain much of their time for real threats.
• Tamper-evident, explained incident records make regulatory reporting and forensic investigation far easier.
• Owning the software means no recurring foreign licence fees, no vendor lock-in, and sensitive network data that never leaves national infrastructure.

4.8 Related work that has succeeded

KAVACH builds on ideas with a solid track record. Meta AI's I-JEPA and V-JEPA showed that predicting in a learned representation space yields robust world models, and NetJEPA applies that idea to network traffic. DreamerV3 (Hafner et al.) showed that systems which learn how their environment behaves and then "imagine" ahead can master a wide range of tasks. Commercial self-learning network detection products already run in thousands of organisations, showing that behavioural modelling works in production; KAVACH adds forecasting, deeper explanations and offline deployment. MITRE ATT&CK is the common language SOCs, CERTs and governments already use, and permissioned blockchains such as Hyperledger Fabric already keep tamper-evident records in government and finance.

4.9 In closing

KAVACH does what the problem statement asks:
• it reads both flow-level and packet-level traffic;
• it represents the network's state as graphs;
• it learns how that state changes, as probabilities;
• it forecasts attacker progression several steps ahead;
• it maps behaviour to MITRE ATT&CK stages;
• it explains every prediction;
• it runs entirely offline;
• it clearly outperforms the logistic-regression baseline on the measures that matter.

It then goes further, with a blockchain trust layer and security that tightens as networks become more sensitive, built around how India's critical infrastructure actually works. We have a working prototype, honest benchmarks and a clear path to national deployment, and we believe KAVACH can move India's cyber defence from reacting to attacks to anticipating them.
